## Your own words 
These questions are designed to test your **understanding of the text**, and your **ability to summarise** it.

For these questions, the wording might include:

- Identify, in your own words as far as possible….
    
- Explain in your own words…
    
- Summarise, in your own words…

### How to approach these questions

Write a summary in your own words, pay attention to all the important details of the question.
You **must** not use the key words used in the passage in your answer.
If they use dates or statistics, you must put these in your own words e.g:
1887 = Victorian age, the late 19$^{\text{th}}$ century, in the past 
58% of adults = more than half of grown ups, just under 60% of older people

### Answer formula
- Bullet points of the main info from the text

## Word Choice
These test your **analytical skills** by asking you to **comment** on **connotations** and **effects**.

For these questions, the wording might include:
- Explain how two examples of the writers word choice makes it clear...
- Explain how the writer's word choice helps to show...

### How to approach these questions 
- **Quote** a single **word** or **phrase** 
- **Explain** what it **suggests, implies or convays** 
- **Link** your comment back to the question

### Answer Formula
Word choice questions are worth 2 marks. Each question is worth 4 marks so you must do **two** word choice analyses
1 mark for quote.
1 mark for connotations + analysis

Your answer **must** follow this bullet pointed structure:
- **"Quote"** 
- 3 Connotations 
- Analysis and link to question.

#### Example:
At the beginning of the month I was in a hellish yet beautiful place. I was making a programme for Radio 4 about one of the worlds most ancient trade routes.
*Show how the writers word choice reveals their opinion about the North African deserts (2)*  
- "Hellish"
- Extremely hot, deadly, cruel
- The use of the word hellish suggests the desert was an evil, hot and life-threatening place. It shows the writer struggles being there.

## Sentence structure
These explore how the **sentence patterns, punctuation or structure shape the meaning of the text** 

For these questions, the wording might include:
- Explain how one example of the writer's sentence structure makes it clear that...
- By referring to one example of sentence structure, explain how...
- Explain how one feature of the writers sentence structure is used to highlight an important point

### How to approach these questions
**Identify** a piece of sentence structure and explain its intended effect or purpose.

## Language questions
These questions are quite broad and may cover **word choice, imagery or tone.** 

For these questions, the wording might include:
- By referring to two examples of language, explain how the writer emphasises...
- Choose one of the two rhetorical questions in paragraph 11 and discuss its effect...

### How to approach these questions
**Identify** examples or word choice or another literary device, give a short quote then analyse the technique and explain its intended effect.

### Answer formulae
#### Imagery
- **"Quote"**
- Just as .... so too ....

##### Example:
"The killer was behind the door, waiting like a spider"
- "Like a spider" = simile
- Just as a spider waits in hiding and traps flies, so too the killer has hidden themselves and is waiting for their victim.

#### Tone
Tone analyses are worth either 2 or 3 marks 
1 mark for quote.
1 mark for correct identification of tone **(if a 2 mark question they will give you the tone)**
1 mark for analysis

Your answer **must** follow this bullet pointed structure:
- **"Quote"**
- Tone (anger, sarcasm, frustration, hopeful, bitter, happy etc)
- Word Choice/imagery/sentence structure analysis

Types of tone:

| Tone            | Intended effect                                                                                       |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tongue-in-cheek | To poke fun at subject                                                                                |
| Matter of fact  | To convey information in a neutral manner                                                             |
| Humorous        | To mock subject; to make light of subject; to draw attention to absubity                              |
| Emotive         | To move reader to sympathy/guilt/anger                                                                |
| Angry           | To share frustration and anger with the reader                                                        |
| Sombre          | To explore a sad or serious event                                                                     |
| Sarcastic       | To ridicule or mock in a harsh manner                                                                 |
| Dismissive      | To try to make the opinion they are showing look stupid, to make people less likely to agree with it. |

##### Example:
*So why, after a decade of phone-in rows, vote-rigging accusation and celebrity hungry wannabes with bloated egos, does the British public remain so in love with reality TV?*
**Explain how the writer conveys a tone of disapproval?(2)**
- "Bloated egos"
- Overstuffed, cocky, over-confident
- The use of "Bloated egos" shows the writer's disapproval of reality TV as they feel the programs are full of attention seekers and cocky people who can't understand who enjoys watching this.

## Link questions
These test whether you understand the **flow of ideas within the passage**.

For these questions, the wording might include:
- Look at line 34, By referring to any part of this sentence, explain how it helps to provide a link between the writer's ideas at this point in the passage.

### How to approach these questions
Quote a **word or short phrase** from the linking sentence that **clearly connects** it to an **earlier or later** idea, **explain** which idea it connects **back or forward** to. Quote or explain how it introduces the next idea.

### Answer formula
- **"Quote"** links back to what the writer said about ...
- **"Quote"** links forward to what the writer says about...

#### Example:
In 1975 "Jaws" set the hearts of its viewers thumping in time to the theme tune. A Great White enjoyed a feeding frenzy on unsuspecting tourists. _Now, however, scientists have discovered facts that suggest the film makers facts are false._ In actual fact the Great White is a fussy eater...
- "the filmmakers" links back to what the writer said about the 1975 film jaws.
- "Facts are false" links forward to what the writer says about the depiction of the sharks in jaws are inaccurate.

## Conclusion questions
These questions show how well you can **recognise** an **effective ending**.

For these questions, the wording might include:
- Look at lines 54 - 32. Select any expression from these lines and explain how it contributes to the passage's effective conclusion

### How to approach these questions
**Quote** a **word or short phrase** from the conclusion that **links back** to a previous point in the text, explain what idea or theme it connects to, then comment on why this is an effective way of closing the passage.
