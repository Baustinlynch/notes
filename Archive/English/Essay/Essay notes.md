The Birds by Daphne Du Maurier is a 1950's apocalyptic novelette set in post WW2 southern England. It follows Nat Hocken and his family as they tackle the sudden change in the weather and a drastic change in the behaviour of the birds around them. In the story, he must defend himself and his family from the relentless attacks of the birds and reconcile with the fact that no one else will believe him about the danger of the birds.

The Motif of the east wind is used throughout the text to foreshadow the attacks of the birds

"The east wind, like a razor, stripped the trees, and the leaves, crackling and dry, shivered and scattered with the winds blast."

The motif of the east wind represents the sudden and violent change that the birds bring. The east wind is a biblical reference; it refers to the east wind that brought the plagues of Egypt to the Pharaoh and his people for their mistreatment of Moses and the Jews. The east wind also symbolises change, renewal, divine power and judgement and new beginnings. But in the story of The Birds, this change is not good for humanity **--Link to the Question !!!--**

Throughout the story, Du Maurier depicts the senseless, indiscriminate, 
calculated yet chaotic violence from the Birds against the humans.

"The terrible, fluttering wings. He could feel the blood on his hands, his wrists, his neck. Each stab of a swooping beak tore his flesh."

This quote shows the way that the birds are unforgiving and have an overwhelming scale, larger than anything that humanity can defend from. At multiple points in the story, Du Maurier refers to the birds in a militaristic manner, which helps to show the way that they are regimented and working towards a common goal, the eradication of the humans.

"but they were not yet defeated, for as again and again they returned to the assault, jabbing his hands, his neck, the little stabbing beaks sharp as pointed forks."

This quote show the way that Du Maurier depicts the birds as if they are a military force, as they were not giving up, sending wave after wave, just as an invading army would after being defeated in battle. **--link to the question--**

Throughout the story, Nat is grappling with the fact that he cannot save all of those around him and that some people just will not believe the threat that the birds pose to them.

"People standing about, shouting and laughing, getting drunk. "Come and watch the birds!""

This quote shows the way that people very easily underestimate the consequences of something that is happening, even if presented with evidence. **--link--**

Throughout the text the symbol of the wireless is used to demonstrate their link to the rest of the country as the slowly realise that no one is coming to save them.

""At half past eight Nat switched the wireless off. (...) There isn't going to be any news." Said Nat. "We've got to depend on ourselves." "

The symbol of the wireless recurs throughout the text and represents stability and human technology. As long as the BBC keep giving out announcements, they know that everything is going to be fine and that the government will come and help them eventually. When they realise that the BBC has stopped broadcasting, it represents the point that they have lost hope, and the point that they realise that they will need to fend for themselves and that no one will be coming to help them. It symbolises the loss of stability and the turning point when they know that nothing will ever be the same.

Throughout the text, Du Maurier uses the symbol of the fag packet to represent hope, human comforts and normality and throughout the story, she uses it as a literary device to represent the loss of them.

"He reached for it, switched on the silent wireless. He threw the empty packet on the fire and watched it burn."

Throughout the text, the fag packet is used to represent human comforts, as it is something that people use for comfort. When Nat burns the fag packet, it symbolises him finally realising that nothing will ever be the same and that the birds are something that he is responsible for and that won't fix itself on its own. **--link to question--**

In conclusion, throughout The Birds, Du Maurier seems to have been intent on showing how easily the safety and security of humanity can be destroyed by the most unforeseen threats. And how even when presented with substantial evidence, people will not believe and respect a threat if it doent line up with how they think things are supposed to go. **-- link back to question --**
