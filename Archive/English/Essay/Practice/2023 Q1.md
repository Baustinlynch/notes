The birds by Daphne Du Maurier is a 1952 apocalyptic novelette set in post-WW2 southern England. It follows Nat Hocken as he has to defend his family from a drastic change in the weather, and an even more drastic change in the behaviour of the birds around him. Throughout the story Nat has to grapple with the fact that he cannot defend all of those around him, and that they will have to fend for themselves, as no one is coming to save them. When Du Maurier wrote this text she designed it as an allegory for the cold war, but i am looking at it through the modern theme of human conflict with nature.

Throughout the birds Du Maurier depicts the senseless, indiscriminate, calculated yet chaotic attacks of the birds against humanity. 

"The terrible, fluttering wings. He could feel the blood on his hands, his wrists, his neck. **Each stab of a swooping beak tore his flesh**"

"**But they were not yet defeated, for as again and again they returned to the assault, jabbing his hands, his neck, the little swooping beaks sharp as pointed forks**"

This quote also shows the way that the birds were acting in a militaristic manner, as the birds were sending in wave after wave of reinforcements, just as an invading army would after being defeated in battle. This links well to the important theme of human conflict with nature; as it portrays the birds as being in an active battle with humanity, prepared, with a strong strategy.

**Throughout the text, Nat is grappling with the fact that he cannot save all of those around him, as some people just will not appreciate the threat that something like the birds poses,** as they just do not believe that the birds can be a threat to them.

**"People standing about, shouting and laughing, getting drunk. "Come and watch the birds!""**

This quote shows the way that people often don't appreciate "non-concrete" threats, and don't feel the need to defend themselves from them. This links well to the theme of human conflict with nature, **as nature is something that is all around us** but we take it for granted; we cannot understand how nature could pose a threat to us.

**The symbol of the wireless** is used throughout the text to represent their connection to the outside world, and the hopeful belief that everything is going to be OK and that someone will come and help them eventually. **As long as the BBC is still broadcasting**, they can be hopeful that they will be saved eventually. **When the BBC has stopped broadcasting,** it marks the turning point when they realise that they will have to fend for themselves and that nothing will ever be the same again. It links to the important theme of human conflict with nature very well as it **symbolises the loss of stability and the point in which humanity has lost in the conflict with the birds.**

Throughout the text, Du Maurier uses the symbol of the fag packet to symbolise human comforts and normality.

"**He reached for it, switched** on the silent wireless. H**e threw the empty packet in the fire and watched it burn**"

The symbol of the fag packet is used to symbolise human comforts and normality throughout the text. When Nat throws the packet into the fire, it represents him admitting that nothing will be the same, and that his and his family's lives will forever be affected. This links well to the theme of human conflict with nature; just as civilians will realise that their home has been invaded successfully, and that they have lost in a conflict with another group of humans, so too has Nat realised that humanity has been defeated by the birds.

In conclusion, throughout The Birds, Du Maurier has been intent on showing us that we, as humanity, can easily be defeated by threats that may seem more disconnected from us than they actually are. When Du Maurier wrote the birds, she meant it to be an allegory for nuclear war, but i have looked at it through the more modern lens of human conflict with nature, but the lessons that it teaches us still stand.
