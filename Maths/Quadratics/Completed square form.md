Not all quadratics are perfect squares. But **any quadratic** of the form $x^2+bx+c$ can be written as **a perfect square** $\pm$ **a number**,
$$y=(x+p)^2+q$$
$$\text{e.g.}$$
$$y=(x+1)^2+4$$
$$y=(x-3)^2+5$$
### Example:
*Rewrite* $y=x^2-6x+11$ *in the form of* $y=(x-b)^2+c$
To get $b$ (the number inside the bracket) you need to **halve the coefficient** (the number in front) of the second term in the original equation
So, for our example half of -6 is -3 so the bracket becomes $(x-3)$.
Outside the bracket we must **subtract the square of our half** since we are putting it in the bracket. So in our case we will subtract 9
$y=(x-3)^2-9+11$
$y=(x-3)^2+2$

This form is used when [[Sketching Quadratic Graphs from Completed Square Form|sketching quadratic graphs]] to read off the turning point directly.
